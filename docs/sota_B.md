# State of the art: communication and teammate selection in cooperative driving

## Scope and project status

This review focuses on whether **teammate selection**—choosing which nearby agents to use as communication partners—affects cooperative-driving performance when communication is constrained. Related work distinguishes this from learning **when** to communicate, **what** information to send, or how to use trust in cooperative decisions; these mechanisms are related but not interchangeable.

The project brief specifies a CARLA 0.9.16 study, with simulated communication range, latency, and packet loss. The repository currently contains a CARLA environment skeleton, not an experimental system: its observations are placeholders, rewards are zero, and V2X and grouping hooks are marked as future work. Therefore, the planned selection comparisons and outcomes below are research plans, not completed implementation or results. The README and environment-spec documentation currently provide no additional research-plan detail.

## Comparative literature review

| Work | Problem and setting | Communication, selection, and learning mechanism | Evaluation and reported findings | Limitation relative to this question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Who2com** [1] | Bandwidth-sensitive collaborative perception among aerial robots in AirSim-CP; the task includes semantic segmentation. It is not a driving-control MARL study. | A multi-stage learned handshake: a target agent sends a compressed request, peers return matching scores, and the target chooses a partner from which to receive information. This is explicit communication-partner selection for perception. | The authors report about 20% higher segmentation accuracy than decentralized baselines and performance comparable to a centralized approach with one quarter of its bandwidth. | Selects perception partners, not driving teammates for policy coordination. The reported task and communication model do not establish robustness to V2X latency or packet loss. |
| **When2com** [2] | Distributed multi-agent perception, evaluated on two perception tasks. | Learns to form communication groups and decide when to communicate. Group formation and communication timing are learned; this is broader than a fixed radius rule but is not itself a driving-control policy. | The authors report reduced communication bandwidth while maintaining superior task performance; the accessible abstract does not give a numeric reduction. | Does not evaluate autonomous-driving safety or driving efficiency, nor isolate teammate selection from communication scheduling in a V2X driving comparison. |
| **ATOC** [3] | Cooperative MARL in non-driving multi-agent scenarios. | Attentional communication learns when communication is useful and how to integrate other agents' information. It adaptively selects relevant teammate information rather than using an all-to-all, fixed communication pattern. | The authors report more coordinated strategies and effective communication across cooperative scenarios. The accessible abstract does not enumerate scenario-level metrics or numeric results. | Establishes an attention-based communication approach, not a CARLA/V2X teammate-selection evaluation. The abstract does not establish performance under network latency or packet loss. |
| **Goal-Oriented MARL for Decentralized Agent Teams** [4] | Decentralized multi-agent navigation with obstacles, dynamic agent populations, partial observability, and limited communication. The authors motivate the method using autonomous vehicles across different domains; this is not evidence of a road-traffic benchmark. | A decentralized MARL framework in which agents communicate selectively using local goals and observations. This is goal-aware communication selection, distinct from trust-based coordination and from a driving-specific communication scheduler. | The authors report increased task success and reduced time-to-goal versus non-cooperative baselines, with stable performance as agent count increases. The accessible abstract gives no numeric effect sizes. | Does not establish a CARLA driving result or a controlled comparison of learned, radius-based, and random partners under V2X impairments. “GoMARL” is not the paper's title or a verified canonical method name; the exact title is used here. |
| **IntNet** [5] | Cooperative autonomous driving in urban environments with autonomous and human-operated vehicles. The accessible abstract does not identify the simulator, so this review does not label it a CARLA study. | Communicates predicted vehicle intent over a vehicle-to-vehicle network. An observation-prediction module and graph-attention network are combined with a learned scheduler that dynamically optimizes the communication graph. This is adaptive communication scheduling, not merely a fixed partner rule. | The authors report lower collision rates and up to 60% lower communication effort than communicative MARL baselines, and assess learning efficiency and scalability. These claims are from the abstract; specific baseline values and scenario-level metric details are not reproduced here. | Integrates communication design with policy learning, so it does not by itself establish the isolated causal effect of teammate-selection strategy under matched driving and network conditions. The accessible abstract does not specify latency or packet-loss tests. |
| **Trust-MARL** [6] | Cooperative on-ramp merging in heterogeneous traffic, including connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) and human-driven vehicles (HVs). This is an arXiv preprint, not treated here as peer-reviewed. | Uses inter-agent trust for macro-level group coordination and a dynamic trust mechanism to adapt CAV cooperation to current and historical behavior. A trust-triggered game-theoretic module guides lane-change cooperation. The abstract describes trust-aware coordination, not selection of communication partners or message reliability. | The abstract reports comparative and ablation experiments across CAV penetration rates and traffic densities, with claimed gains in safety, efficiency, comfort, and adaptability. It provides no numerical effects in the abstract. | Addresses trust and merge decisions rather than teammate-selection alternatives under simulated V2X range, latency, or packet loss. Simulator and detailed metric values are not asserted here because they were not verified from the accessible abstract. |

### MARL training context

The following methods are useful algorithmic context for cooperative training, but do not answer the teammate-selection question: their central contribution is how agents learn a joint policy or value, not which communication partners should be selected.

| Method | Learning contribution and evaluated setting | Why it does not resolve the selection question |
|---|---|---|
| **VDN** [7] | Decomposes a team action-value into per-agent values for cooperative learning from a shared reward; evaluates partially observable cooperative multi-agent domains. | Value decomposition is a learning/credit-assignment mechanism, not a communication-partner policy. |
| **QMIX** [8] | Uses a state-conditioned monotonic mixing network over local agent values for centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE); evaluated on StarCraft II micromanagement tasks. | CTDE and value mixing do not specify which agents communicate or model V2X constraints. |
| **MAPPO** [9] | Studies PPO-based cooperative MARL, including centralized-training/decentralized-execution variants; evaluates particle-world, StarCraft multi-agent, Google Research Football, and Hanabi testbeds. | It is a useful policy-learning baseline, but the paper does not isolate communication-partner selection. |

## 5.1 Main research directions

- **Learning whom to communicate with:** Who2com explicitly selects a perception partner through a learned handshake. When2com learns communication groups, while ATOC uses attention to select and integrate useful teammate information. These methods demonstrate learned selection or grouping in perception and general cooperation, not necessarily driving.
- **Learning when or what to communicate:** When2com and ATOC adapt communication timing or information use. IntNet schedules communication dynamically and sends predicted vehicle intent. Selection, scheduling, and message content are separate design choices.
- **Communication-aware cooperative driving:** IntNet is directly relevant because it combines learned communication with cooperative driving and reports safety and communication-effort outcomes. Its abstract does not establish the effect of selection alone under explicit latency and packet-loss conditions.
- **Trust-aware heterogeneous coordination:** Trust-MARL uses trust to adapt coordination and lane-change decisions in mixed traffic. Trust can influence how an agent cooperates without necessarily determining which communication partner it selects.

## 5.2 What existing methods establish

The reviewed evidence shows that learned partner choice, group formation, attention, goal-conditioned communication, adaptive scheduling, and trust-aware decisions can each support cooperation in their respective settings. The evidence spans perception, generic cooperative tasks, decentralized navigation, and urban driving; it therefore does not imply that these methods are interchangeable or directly comparable. Their observations, tasks, baselines, communication assumptions, and reported metrics differ. In particular, results from perception accuracy or task completion do not substitute for collision rate, travel time, time-to-collision (TTC), comfort, or network overhead in a controlled driving experiment.

## 5.3 Remaining experimental question

The question investigated here is narrower than whether communication helps: **does the teammate-selection strategy itself change cooperative-driving safety and efficiency under simulated V2X constraints?** In the reviewed sources, communication mechanisms are commonly evaluated as part of a broader perception or policy system. The evidence reviewed here does not provide a matched CARLA comparison of learned/intelligent, radius-based, and random teammate selection while holding the driving task and other experimental factors constant. This is a scoped observation about the reviewed evidence, not a claim that no prior work has studied the question.

## 5.4 How the planned experiment addresses it

The planned CARLA study should hold the driving task, reward, candidate-neighbor information, network assumptions, and evaluation scenarios constant while varying only teammate-selection strategy: radius-based, random, and learned attention-based selection. Simulated range, latency, and packet loss should be varied as explicit conditions only once they are implemented. Outcomes should include collision rate, success rate, travel time, TTC, comfort/jerk, and communication overhead; logging selected teammate IDs and communication statistics makes selection behavior auditable. This controlled design can help distinguish selection effects from effects caused by a different policy, task, or communication budget. These are planned measures, not current CARLA results.

## 5.5 Research hypotheses

- **H1:** Learned attention-based selection may improve safety and/or efficiency relative to random selection when relevant teammate information is available.
- **H2:** Its advantage over radius-based selection may depend on traffic density and communication quality, including latency and packet loss.
- **H3:** Any safety or efficiency gains may involve a trade-off in communication overhead or selection complexity.

These are falsifiable hypotheses. No method is assumed to outperform another.

## Research gap and defensible contribution

Prior work addresses learned perception partners, communication grouping and scheduling, goal-aware information exchange, communication-driven cooperative driving, and trust-aware merging. What remains to be established for this project is the effect of teammate-selection strategy under matched cooperative-driving and simulated V2X conditions. The planned study will compare learned attention-based selection with radius-based and random selection in CARLA, measuring safety, efficiency, comfort, and communication outcomes across network conditions. If supported by controlled experiments, its defensible contribution would be an empirical characterization of the trade-offs among these selection strategies—not a claim to the first such method or to guaranteed superiority.
